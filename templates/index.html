Decision‑preserving
abstraction

Every project below is a different surface of one question: when is it safe to treat two different things as the same thing? And its mirror: what is the smallest representation that preserves the difference when it matters?

{{COUNTS}}

The program

Suppose a system has states x ∈ X, and its downstream behaviour or decision is represented by F(x). Define an equivalence relation

x1 ∼F x2  ⇔  F(x1) = F(x2)

Then the system can operate on equivalence classes

X / ∼F

instead of retaining every original state. For a single fixed decision this is straightforward. The research challenge is making it work when decisions depend on future trajectories, constraints, costs and changing environments — which is to say, when the equivalence has to be justified rather than assumed.

How can we construct minimal, efficient representations that preserve all decision-relevant information under specified constraints?

This is sufficient statistics, state abstraction, bisimulation, automata minimisation and information theory, treated as one subject rather than five. The projects below explore both sides of it.

Merge when it is safe

FUTCache does not need to remember every state it has visited; it needs a compact representation that preserves what matters about explored geometry.

Refuse to merge

Phantom Clock: two rays can occupy the same spatial position and still differ in direction and arrival time. A memory that treats them as identical manufactures timing errors.

Define legitimacy

Behavioral Quotients asks when two different computational histories may be treated as equivalent at all.

Route on what remains

Model routing: two prompts may look similar yet require different models, and two very different prompts may be served equally well by the same cheap one. The capability required matters, not superficial similarity.

Guard the distinctions

Authorization Lattice and Agent Guardrail: two agents may request the same action while holding different permissions. Ignoring that distinction is a security failure.
